# Pedagogy & Methodology: Neural Nets, Through One Toy Task

## How to read this document

This is the companion to `experiment_design.md`. The design doc tells you *what* to
run; this one explains *why*, building neural-network fundamentals from the ground up
using our single toy task as the running example. It is meant to be re-read — probably
more than once — and to serve as a launching point for deeper "learning" sessions
later.

The task throughout: **given a generals.io frame, output the index of the alive player
with the smallest total army.**

---

## The anchor: hold this picture the entire time

> The ideal solution is **`score_p = -army_p`, then `argmax`.**

As a single linear layer `Linear(8, 8)`, that is one 8×8 matrix of numbers, and the
correct value of that matrix is **W = -I** — the negated identity: each output copies
its own player's army, negated, ignoring the other seven. The final `argmax` then
picks the player whose negated army is largest, i.e. whose army is smallest.

**The entire "correct" network for the clean case is one 8×8 matrix that training
should drive toward -I.** Every architecture in the menu is just a differently-shaped
*container* for functions, and for each one you ask three questions:

1. **Can** the container even hold the `-army` solution? (representational capacity)
2. Can it hold it **cleanly** — as the obvious unique answer, not one of many fits?
   (inductive bias / identifiability)
3. Will **SGD actually find** it from the data? (optimization)

Most "big decisions" in the design are really "which of those three am I stressing."
Keep returning to the `-I` picture; everything else is variation on "how do we get to
W ≈ -I, and what happens when the task won't fit in a single matrix."

---

## Decision 1 — What a single layer does

A **linear layer** `Linear(in, out)` is just a matrix multiply plus an offset:

```
out = W · x + b
```

`W` is a grid of numbers (the *weights*) that training adjusts; `b` is the *bias*.
`Linear(8, 8)` takes 8 per-player army values and produces 8 scores, where **every
output is a weighted sum of all 8 inputs.** So `W` is 8×8 = **64 free numbers.**

The `-army` solution is `W = -I`: output *p* = `-1 × army_p + 0 × (everyone else)`.
That's the whole clean network. Hold that.

---

## Decision 2 — Collapsing the board (pooling), and which pooling op

Our input is not 8 numbers; it is `[8, H, W]` — each player's one army number
**smeared across the whole board** as a constant. Before a `Linear(8,8)` can act on
"8 numbers," something has to collapse H×W to one value per channel. That collapse is
**pooling**, and the choice of op is a real decision here, not a detail:

- **Average pool** — mean over all H×W positions in a channel. Since each channel is
  constant, the mean *is* that constant: **lossless, free, perfect.** This is why
  "pool-first → Linear(8,8)" is the canonical clean net.
- **Max pool** — the maximum. On a constant channel, max also returns the constant —
  *also* lossless here. But max becomes interesting under the alive encodings: with a
  -1 sentinel, a dead player's channel is all -1, and max **ignores low values**, so
  max-pool quietly does something different from avg-pool once sentinels/masks enter.
  That is why we sweep avg vs max **only** crossed with the sentinel/mask encodings.
- **Flatten** — *refuses* to collapse. It lays all 8·H·W numbers out as one long
  vector and feeds the whole thing onward. Now the network has thousands of inputs and
  must **learn** that all positions inside one channel are the same number and should
  be treated identically. It has no built-in notion of that redundancy. This is the
  hard road, and that is the point: the gap between pool-first and flatten measures
  **how much the right inductive bias matters.**

Two axes fall out, and both only became interesting *because* of the broadcast format:
**pooling location** (before vs after the learnable layer) and **pooling op** (avg vs
max).

---

## Decision 3 — Weight sharing across players (the conceptual heart)

This is the richest axis and the least standard, so go slowly.

**The key fact:** `score_p = -army_p` depends *only on player p's own army.* No player
needs to look at any other player to compute their score — the comparison happens
entirely in the final `argmax`. So the function treats all 8 players **identically**:
whatever rule maps "my army" → "my score," it is the **same rule for everyone.**

The formal name is *permutation equivariance*, but forget the name and hold the
mechanical picture:

> **The same little function, applied 8 times, once per player.**

Now, three ways to build a network, in increasing order of how hard you *force* it to
respect that symmetry. This is the **equivariance ladder.**

### Rung 1 — Unconstrained `Linear(8, 8)` (64 free numbers)

Nothing stops output-3 from depending on player-5's army. The symmetry is *allowed*
(W = -I respects it) but **not required** — SGD could land on some lopsided 64-number
matrix that happens to fit your training set and then **breaks when you shuffle player
order.**

The experiment: *does training discover the symmetry on its own, or do you have to
feed it order-shuffled data to nudge it there?*

### Rung 2 — Equivariant linear (2 numbers)

Mathematically restrict `W` to only the matrices that treat players identically. It
turns out there are exactly two such building blocks, combined as:

```
score = a · (own army) + b · (sum of everyone's army)
```

— i.e. **2 numbers** instead of 64. The `-army` solution is **a = -1, b = 0.** You've
collapsed a 64-dimensional search into a 2-dimensional one by baking in the symmetry.
Far less to learn, far less that can go wrong, and it **cannot break under permutation
by construction.**

(Concretely, the only S₈-symmetric 8×8 matrices are `a·I + b·11ᵀ` — a scaled identity
plus a scaled all-ones matrix. -I is the `a=-1, b=0` member.)

### Rung 3 — Shared per-player head (φ)

Instead of one matrix over all 8 players, write **one** small function φ and apply that
*same* φ **independently to each player's features** to get their score. φ might be
"negate the scalar," or a tiny MLP. This is the most explicit form of "same rule, 8
times." It is also the most **sample-efficient** (almost nothing to learn), and the
only rung that would extend to a variable number of players (we're fixed at 8, so
that's not our motivation — but it's the cleanest expression of the symmetry).

### Why the ladder is the scientific spine

You can **measure where symmetry comes from.** Train the unconstrained `Linear(8,8)`,
inspect the learned matrix, and ask: *did it land in the `a·I + b·11ᵀ` family?*

- **Yes, on its own** → SGD found the symmetry from data alone.
- **Only with order augmentation** → symmetry came from the data pipeline.
- **Only when hard-constrained (rungs 2–3)** → symmetry had to come from architecture.

Same task, three possible sources of the same structure. That is a genuinely clean,
*measurable* version of the vague question "can nets learn this."

And the `DeepSets-with-context-branch` net is a deliberate overkill on top of rung 3:
it adds a pooled-context branch the task doesn't need, then you watch whether it
**learns to ignore** that branch — direct evidence the task is context-free.

## Decision 4 — Depth and nonlinearity (the notch-maker)

Everything in Decisions 1–3 is **linear.** And stacking linear layers gains you
nothing: matrix times matrix is just another matrix, so two linear layers collapse to
one. **Depth alone is useless.**

What makes depth matter is the **activation function** between layers — almost always
**ReLU**, which is simply:

```
ReLU(x) = max(0, x)      # pass positives through, clamp negatives to zero
```

That one kink is what lets a network *bend.* Stacking bent pieces lets it approximate
any shape — including **non-monotonic** ones.

### Why this is exactly what masking needs

Recall the trap under 0-overload encoding: a dead player has `army = 0`, the
**smallest** value, so plain argmin **maximally wants to pick the dead player** — but
you must *exclude* them. So the desired `score(army)` curve is weird:

- as army drops toward 0, score should **rise** (smaller army = more likely the min =
  higher score),
- but at `army = 0` it must **crash** (dead — never pick).

That is a **non-monotonic notch**: up, then a cliff down.

> A single linear layer literally **cannot** draw that shape — a line is monotonic.
> A one-hidden-layer ReLU MLP **can**: a couple of ReLU kinks build the
> ramp-up-then-cliff.

So **depth + ReLU is precisely the capability** that lets a network solve the
encodings the linear net cannot. This is *why* the depth axis and the encoding axis
must be read as a grid: depth only "earns its keep" exactly where the encoding made
the function nonlinear (0-overload, -1-overload). On all-alive and explicit-mask, the
function is linear and depth is wasted capacity.

### The 0.001-gap wrinkle (a real, predictable difficulty)

A *living* player always holds at least their general, so the smallest live
`total_army` is ≥ 1/1000 = **0.001**, while a dead player is **0.000**. The notch must
fit inside that **0.001-wide band.** It is representable, but **numerically delicate** —
the net has to resolve a very fine scale. This predicts that 0-overload will be the
**most data-hungry** and **most normalization-sensitive** encoding, and it motivates
the **rescaled-gap** variant (widen the band) to separate "encoding is bad" from
"scaling is bad."

This is also the one place where **activation choice** might matter: fitting a sharp
cliff inside a tiny band is the kind of thing where a smooth activation (GELU, tanh)
vs ReLU's hard hinge *could* differ in how reliably SGD lands it. Expectation: small to
nil on a task this simple — which is itself a fine thing to document.

---

## Decision 5 — Width (capacity vs optimization ease)

**Width** = how many units in a hidden layer (the 16 / 32 / 64 numbers). More width =
more ReLU kinks = ability to approximate more complex shapes — *and*, somewhat
counterintuitively, **easier optimization**: extra redundant units give SGD more paths
to a good solution, so it gets stuck less often.

For our task the *required* width is tiny (the notch needs only a handful of kinks), so
width mostly tests **"does over-provisioning help SGD find the answer faster / more
reliably,"** not "do we need the capacity." Expect **diminishing-to-flat returns** —
itself a clean negative result on a task this simple. That's why width is a fixed value
(32) for main runs and at most an optional secondary curve, not a grid axis.

---

## Why convolution earns nothing here

A convolution slides a kernel over the board computing **local weighted sums.** But on
a **constant** channel every location is the same number, so a 3×3 conv returns
`(Σ kernel weights) × constant`, identical at every interior pixel. It collapses to a
scaled **1×1 conv**, which is just a per-channel linear scaling. **Depth and kernel
size buy zero extra representational power.** A "deep CNN" on this input is
representationally no stronger than a single matrix — the spatial machinery has nothing
to chew on.

So convolutions appear in the menu exactly **once**, in a degenerate role (the
`1×1 conv → pool` restatement of the linear baseline), and their *absence* is one of
the cleaner results: it directly demonstrates that the broadcast encoding **threw away
any spatial structure** for a conv to exploit.

**Crucially, this is a property of the input *format*, not a universal claim.** Feed
raw per-tile army × owner mask instead of the precomputed broadcast total, and the net
must do the spatial summation itself — *there*, conv + global-pool is the natural
correct architecture, and kernel/depth start to matter. That is the bridge toward the
real aux head.

## Methodology principles (why the experiment is shaped as it is)

### Analytic labels → exploit infinite data

Because the ground truth is computable, you can label frames infinitely and cheaply.
This is what makes **sample-complexity curves** (accuracy vs #frames) and
**extrapolation tests** (train one regime, eval another) first-class tools rather than
afterthoughts. Use them: they are how you distinguish "learned the function" from
"memorized the training regime."

### Measure the function, not just the score

A single accuracy number hides the interesting structure. Three sharper lenses:

- **Error vs margin** — errors should pile up where the smallest/second-smallest gap is
  tiny. The shape of this curve tells you how *sharp* a comparator the net actually
  learned. (The margin distribution is bimodal — opening near-ties vs late-game
  blowouts — so sampling phase dominates; be deliberate about it.)
- **Weight inspection** — on linear models, confirm W ≈ -I; on the unconstrained net,
  check whether it landed in the symmetric subspace. The weights *are* the experiment
  for the symmetry question.
- **Tie-aware accuracy** — full-set vs strict-margin vs in-set. A tie is a genuinely
  ambiguous frame; a naive hard-argmax metric punishes the net for picking the "wrong"
  equal player. Strict-margin accuracy is the honest comparator measure; in-set
  accuracy credits identifying *a* correct minimum.

### Failures are about encoding and symmetry, not capacity

The recurring lesson the whole design is built to expose:

> The toy net can always *represent* the answer. When it fails, it is because (a) the
> **encoding** made the function nonlinear (the masking notch) when the architecture
> couldn't bend, or (b) the **architecture** didn't match the player symmetry and SGD
> settled on a brittle asymmetric fit.

Capacity is almost never the bottleneck on a task this small. Keep that framing when
reading every result — and carry it into the harder aux-head investigation, where the
same three questions (can it represent / can it represent cleanly / can SGD find it)
still apply, just with spatial aggregation back in play.

## Glossary (quick reference)

- **Logits / scores** — the raw output numbers before `argmax`/softmax; the model's
  per-player "preference."
- **`argmax` / `argmin`** — index of the largest / smallest element.
- **Linear layer** — `W·x + b`; a matrix multiply plus offset.
- **Pooling** — collapsing a spatial dimension to a summary (avg = mean, max =
  maximum).
- **ReLU** — `max(0, x)`; the kink that lets networks bend.
- **Activation** — the nonlinearity between layers (ReLU, GELU, tanh, …).
- **Width** — units per hidden layer.
- **Depth** — number of (hidden) layers.
- **Permutation equivariance** — relabeling the players relabels the outputs the same
  way; "same rule applied per player."
- **Inductive bias** — structure built into an architecture that biases *which*
  solutions it finds (e.g. pooling assumes positions are interchangeable).
- **Sample complexity** — how much data a model needs to reach a given accuracy.
- **Extrapolation** — performance outside the training distribution's regime.
- **Notch** — the non-monotonic score curve (rise toward small army, crash at the dead
  value) required by overload encodings.
- **DeepSets** — architecture for set inputs: shared per-element encoder, optional
  pooled context, per-element (and/or pooled) output.
