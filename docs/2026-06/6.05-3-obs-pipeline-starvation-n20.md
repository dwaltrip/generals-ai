# Obs-pipeline starvation — the N=20 dense-history slowdown (`6.06-1`)

**Date:** 2026-06-06

**Status:** Producer-side starvation identified as the cause; the largest fix landed (`build_obs` single-pass assembly). A ~22% residual gap at n=20 remains, characterized but not closed (mechanism likely memory bandwidth, not yet pinned). GPU-util sidecar fixed + a startup resource banner added (both permanent). Follow-ups listed at the end.

## TL;DR

- In the dense-history N sweep (n=5/10/20), the n=20 cell trained at **~half** the samples/sec of n=5/n=10 — and n=5≈n=10 then a sharp **cliff** at n=20, not a gradual slope.
- Root cause: **CPU-side obs-pipeline starvation** — the DataLoader workers can't build observations fast enough to keep the GPU fed (n=20 GPU measured at 61% util while n=5 sat near 97%). The usual suspects — GPU compute, GPU memory, container resources, tenancy — are ruled out below.
- The per-sample obs **assembly** cost scaled **super-linearly** with channel count. `build_obs` assembled each sample in three full passes (`np.stack` → `.astype(float32)` → pad-copy); collapsing that to **one write pass into a preallocated padded buffer** recovered most of the gap: n=20 **1,510 → 2,100 sps** (0.56× → 0.78× of n=5), byte-identical output.
- The residual ~22% gap is **likely shared-resource-bound**: raising `num_workers` 8→12 made it *worse* (more contention, deeper dips), so it is not core-bound. Leading suspect is **memory bandwidth**; the leading candidate lever is **fp16 obs**.
- Two instrumentation gaps were closed: the GPU-util sidecar had **never** produced data (missing `nvidia-ml-py`), and there was no record of the container's resource envelope. Both fixed.

## How the cliff works: throughput = min(producer, GPU)

Throughput is `min(producer_rate, GPU_rate)`. The GPU rate is ~flat in N (FLOPs +0.75%). The producer rate *falls* as N grows (bigger tensors to build/move). While the producer is faster than the GPU (n5, n10), throughput sits flat at the GPU ceiling and the producer's slowdown is **hidden**. The moment the producer drops *below* the GPU (n20), throughput follows the producer down. The "cliff" is just the crossover — the underlying producer slowdown is gradual; `min()` makes it look like a wall.

## What it is NOT (ruled out, each with evidence)

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| GPU compute | ❌ | params 11.15M→11.20M, FLOPs/sample 27.95→28.16 (+0.75%) |
| GPU memory / allocator thrash | ❌ | `mem_reserved` 25.3 vs 25.5 GiB (flat), far from the 80 GiB card |
| Container CPU / shm / RAM | ❌ | banner shows 17 cores, 40 GiB /dev/shm, 1 TiB RAM — abundant |
| Tenancy / unlucky host | ❌ | no sign of it — n=5 and n=20 ran on *identical* container specs, back-to-back, and n=20 still halved |
| Consumer (pin_memory / H2D) | ❌ | H2D is `non_blocking=True` + pinned (overlaps compute); pin thread far faster than the batch rate |
| Per-sample obs-build (algorithmic) | ❌ | local profiler flat in N (+3%) — but see the caveat below |
| **Producer obs-pipeline** | ✅ | the only thing left; the fix confirmed the pipeline is the bottleneck (the memory-bandwidth *mechanism* is inferred) |

Caveat: a local M1 obs-build profiler showed obs assembly flat in N (+3%), which initially argued against the producer. **That result did not transfer** — the effect is a cache/memory-bandwidth one, and M1's cache hierarchy and ~400 GB/s unified memory are nothing like Modal's server CPU. Lesson: cache/bandwidth behavior does not port across CPUs; measure on the target.

## Key measurements

Original sweep (3 epochs, steady-state epoch-2; GPU util from Modal's dashboard since the sidecar was broken):

| N | channels | sps | GPU util |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 96 | ~2,396 | ~85–90% |
| 10 | 106 | ~2,360 | ~85–90% |
| 20 | 126 | ~1,233 | ~50% |

Quick probe (1 epoch, 800 batches, on a 17-core H100 box) — before vs after the `build_obs` fix, with the now-working sidecar:

| run | sps | mean util | median | std | mem_reserved |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n=5 | 2,680 | 97% | 96 | 9 | 25.3 GiB |
| n=20 (pre-fix) | 1,510 | 61% | 70 | — | 25.5 GiB |
| n=20 (post-fix) | 2,100 | 76% | 85 | 25 | 25.5 GiB |
| n=20 (post-fix, 12 workers) | 1,900 | 69% | 79 | 32 | — |

Per-worker producer cost for n=20 was 5.30 ms/sample pre-fix and 3.81 ms post-fix (both measured — n=20 was producer-bound in both). n=5's is ~2.9 ms but GPU-capped, so a floor (true cost is lower). Against the +31% channel increase, the n=20 overhead went from **≥+80%** (super-linear) to **≈+31%** (now roughly linear) — i.e. the super-linear component is gone, leaving the proportional "more channels = more bytes."

## The cache-cliff hypothesis (why super-linear, and why the fix is non-linear)

Per-sample obs arrays are small (~0.4–0.5 MiB padded), but the 3-pass assembly kept several live at once. Estimated peak working sets (computed from array shapes; median board ~754 tiles, padded 32×32, fp32):

- n=5: ~950 KiB (stack temp + astype copy + padded buffer + channel list)
- n=20: ~1.25 MiB

A typical server-CPU L2 is ~1–2 MiB/core. If so, n=5 would **fit** L2 while n=20 **spills** to RAM — the kind of step change that turns +31% bytes into ≥+80% time. The single-pass fix drops the two redundant unpadded buffers, shrinking the working set to ~667 KiB (n=5) / ~875 KiB (n=20) — pulling n=20 back under that boundary, which would explain why the win is more than "33% fewer copies." (The L2 boundary is the *hypothesis* — we have not measured Modal's cache; the *measured* fact is the ≥+80%→≈+31% per-sample scaling change after the fix.)

## The fix that landed

`build_obs` (`training/bc/obs.py`, lines 1032 - 1042): three full passes over `[C,H,W]` (`np.stack(channels).astype(float32)` → `np.zeros` → pad-copy) replaced by one write pass into a single preallocated padded buffer (`obs[i,:H,:W] = ch` per channel).

Byte-identical (the slice assignment dtype-converts exactly as `.astype` did); the profiler mirror in `scripts/profile_encode_frame.py` was updated to match.

Verification: sha1-for-sha1 identical over 900 real frames across n=5 and n=20; `test_obs` green. A local micro-bench put the assembly step ~35% faster on M1 — the single write pass wins even where memory is cheap — and end-to-end Modal throughput rose as shown above.

## Residual gap: not core-bound; likely shared-resource-bound

Post-fix n=20 is **bursty**: median util 85% (often near-saturated, p90=100%) but std 25 with frequent deep dips (below 80% nearly half the time). Raising `num_workers` 8→12 on the same 17-core box made it **worse** (mean util 76%→69%, std 25→32, sps 2,100→1,900). Adding producers added contention, not throughput — consistent with a **shared resource** (likely memory bandwidth) being the limit, and with the 6.05-1 finding that per-worker efficiency degrades past a few workers (NUMA/bandwidth). Cores are not the bottleneck; the evidence points to bytes/bandwidth.

(Cold-volume I/O is a natural guess for the dips, but isn't supported: it wouldn't explain why *more* workers — more concurrent producers — made things worse.)

## Instrumentation added (use these going forward)

- **GPU-util sidecar fixed.** `torch.cuda.utilization()` needs the `nvidia-ml-py` NVML backend, which was never a dependency — every prior run's `gpu_util.jsonl` was a single error line. Added the dep and split the sampler loop so torch-native memory (`mem_alloc`/`mem_reserved`) keeps logging even if util ever fails again. Sidecar now produces real `gpu_util_pct` + `mem_reserved_mb`.
- **Startup resource banner.** `training/shared/resource_info.py`, logged once from `build_dataloader`: CPU count, `/dev/shm`, cgroup memory limit, system RAM, GPU, and the DataLoader's in-flight host-memory footprint (per-batch obs bytes × prefetch depth). Makes under-provisioning / starvation risk visible on line one of `run.log`.
- **Container envelope observed.** In these runs, Modal GPU functions with no `cpu=`/`memory=` set landed generous containers: **17 cores, 40 GiB /dev/shm, 1 TiB RAM** for an H100 — so provisioning was not the issue here (not a guarantee, but the default was ample). A no-GPU `modal shell` proxy is *not* representative (it reported 1 core / 16 GiB shm).

## Open questions / next steps

- **fp16 obs (leading remaining lever).** Build/ship obs as fp16 to halve every host-side byte (per-channel allocs, assembly, worker→main IPC, pin copy, H2D) — aimed at the suspected memory-bandwidth wall. Channel value-ranges were checked as fp16-safe by reading each encoding (not yet numerically verified). Requires gating on the fp16 path: the fp32/MPS path runs the model *without* autocast, so fp16 obs into fp32 weights would dtype-clash. Needs a numerics parity check. Lowest-effort variant: preallocate the (already single-pass) assembly buffer as fp16, downcasting channels on the write — keeps the builders fp32 but makes buffer + IPC + pin + H2D fp16.
- **Continue obs-build perf / memory.** ~45 of 86 base channels are `np.full((H,W), scalar)` — a single value materialized across the grid; injecting them as conv bias / FiLM would avoid the materialization entirely (model change). Deeper: build channels directly into the preallocated buffer to drop the per-channel allocations too.
- **Profile the pipeline ON Modal across N.** We inferred producer scaling from training sps; we never ran `profile_encode_frame.py` or the dataloader Probe B on the actual H100 container at n=5/10/20. That would give the real per-region producer curve (build vs collate vs IPC) on the target CPU and confirm the bandwidth diagnosis.
- **Whole-pipeline smoothing.** Examine shuffle buffer, collate, and worker→main IPC for other byte-movement wins or ways to damp the burstiness.
- **prefetch_factor (untested, low confidence).** A deeper prefetch buffer could absorb transient producer dips. Skepticism is warranted since more workers made things worse, but it's cheap to test and targets the burstiness specifically rather than aggregate throughput.
- **Warm ground truth.** The quick probes are 1-epoch. A 2-epoch run (read epoch-2 sps) would give the true steady-state n=20 gap before investing in fp16. Slightly speculative, given the consistency in sps numbers after the first 500 batches or so of epoch 1.
