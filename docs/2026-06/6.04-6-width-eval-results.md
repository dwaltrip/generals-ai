## Setup

Three pairwise matchups between the 0.5× (PoC), 0.75×, and 1× (DeepNash paper) width checkpoints from the 2026-05-31 width sweep. All E3 (final epoch), all pyramid value head.

Protocol adapted from the 5.28 pyramid-vs-direct eval:
- **80 games per matchup** (40 random maps × swap-slots), 240 games total
- **temp=0.5** — the 5.28 sweep established this as the cleanest signal-to-noise ratio for architectural A/B testing
- **Shared map seed** (42) across all three matchups, so the same 40 maps appear in every pair
- No self-vs-self baselines (5.28 validated slot symmetry under swap-slots)
- Per-tick diagnostics collected (value placement, policy entropy, pass probs)

Policy spec: `checkpoint:<path>:temperature=0.5,sample,value_head_variant=pyramid`

Script: `packages/eval-tools/scripts/width_eval_2026-06-04.sh`. Run time: ~24 min on MPS (M1 Max).

## Win rates

| Matchup | W (favored) | N (decided) | Rate | 95% Wilson CI | p (one-sided) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 0.75× vs 0.5× | 42 | 77 | 54.5% | 43.5%–65.2% | 0.247 |
| **0.75× vs 1×** | **48** | **79** | **60.8%** | **49.7%–70.8%** | **0.036** |
| 0.5× vs 1× | 39 | 75 | 52.0% | 40.9%–62.9% | 0.409 |

**Ranking: 0.75× > 0.5× ≈ 1×.** Only the 0.75× vs 1× result is statistically significant (p < 0.05). The other two matchups have CIs that comfortably straddle 50%. At n~80 decided games, a ~60/40 split is detectable but a ~55/45 is not.

The ranking is consistent with the loss-metric ranking from the training sweep, where 0.75× won on every metric and the 1× cell regressed (val_policy: 0.75× 1.094, 0.5× 1.183, 1× 1.161).

## Loss-vs-win-rate tracking

The width eval shows a tighter correspondence between loss gaps and win rates than the 5.28 pyramid-vs-direct eval:

| Pair | val_policy gap | Win rate (better model) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.75× vs 0.5× | 7.6% (1.183 → 1.094) | 55% |
| 0.75× vs 1× | 5.8% (1.161 → 1.094) | 61% |
| 0.5× vs 1× | 1.9% (1.183 → 1.161) | 52% |

In the pyramid-vs-direct case, a 1.5% val_policy gap amplified to ~13pp win rate — "nearly an order of magnitude." Here the ranking is monotonic and the amplification factor is much lower. The likely explanation: the pyramid-vs-direct difference involved a qualitative behavioral shift (pass rate halving due to trunk regularization from a different value-head architecture), while the width variants are the same architecture at different capacities — differences in loss map more directly to differences in the same decision space.

## Pass rate

| Width | Pass rate |
|---|---|
| 0.5× | ~6.5% |
| 0.75× | ~9.5% |
| 1× | ~21% |

(Consistent across matchups — each model's pass rate is stable regardless of opponent.)

The 1× model wastes roughly 1 in 5 turns passing. This is the largest behavioral difference across the three widths. Consistent with undertraining: the trunk hasn't learned strong enough features for the pass head to stay low. Despite this, 1× still trades roughly even with 0.5×, suggesting its capacity compensates for the wasted turns.

For reference, the pyramid-vs-direct behavioral gap was 15% vs 33% — similar magnitude, different root cause (trunk regularization vs undertraining).

## Policy entropy

| Width | Mean entropy | Low-conf ticks | High-conf ticks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5× | 1.31 | 14.8% | 35.2% |
| 0.75× | 1.30 | 14.6% | 32.5% |
| 1× | 1.47 | 23.9% | 34.5% |

The 1× model is measurably less decisive — higher entropy, nearly 1 in 4 move decisions made at low confidence. The 0.5× and 0.75× models are essentially identical in decision sharpness. Corroborates the pass rate finding: the 1× trunk hasn't converged to sharp policy features.

## Map-level consistency

All three matchups used the same 40 random maps (seed 42); 37 unique replay IDs appear in all three result sets (3 maps were sampled twice by the RNG, producing 4 games each instead of 2).

**0.75× outcomes across opponents:**

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| win | 3 | 7 | 1 | 11 |
| split | 7 | 9 | 2 | 18 |
| loss | 2 | 5 | 1 | 8 |
| **total** | **12** | **21** | **4** | **37** |

**1× outcomes across opponents:**

| vs 0.5× ↓ \ vs 0.75× → | win | split | loss | total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| win | 1 | 3 | 1 | 5 |
| split | 3 | 14 | 7 | 24 |
| loss | 0 | 4 | 4 | 8 |

Both tables are dominated by splits (21/37 maps). 1× won against both opponents on only 1 map, lost to both on 4. There is no "1× shines on complex maps" pocket — its losses are broad-based, and the 0.75× advantage comes from winning slightly more marginal maps across the board.

The 1× model's wins skew long — median 449 ticks vs ~395 for the other two, with a third of its wins going past 600 ticks. It grinds to victory rather than closing out quickly. The 0.5× model has the fastest wins (12% under 200 ticks). One reading: 1×'s extra capacity helps in extended games where there's more time for deeper features to matter, even though those features are undertrained. This is the most suggestive signal that 1× with more training could improve.

## Summary

0.75× is the clear winner at this training budget. It beats 1× with statistical significance (p=0.036) and edges 0.5× directionally across win rate, loss metrics, and behavioral indicators.

The 1× regression is an undertraining effect, supported by converging evidence:
- **High pass rate** (21% vs 6–10%) — wasting turns
- **High policy entropy** (1.47 vs 1.30) — indecisive moves
- **No map-level pockets of strength** — losses are broad, not map-type-specific
- **Long-game wins** — the one place 1× shows relative strength is in extended games, suggesting its capacity can matter when given time

Whether 1× with a scaled training budget (more epochs, tuned LR) would overtake 0.75× remains open. The long-game signal is a soft hint that it might, but nothing here justifies delaying the big run on 0.75× to find out.
