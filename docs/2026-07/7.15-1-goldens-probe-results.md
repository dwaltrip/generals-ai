# Goldens size-and-runtime probe — results

*Date: 2026-07-15. The empirical record of the size-and-runtime probe (pre-build item 3 in [7.10-2 §9](7.10-2-behavior-goldens-first-cut-design.md), step 2 of the [7.12-5 handoff](7.12-5-goldens-prebuild-handoff.md)), run against five corpus fixtures. This doc records measurements and findings only. The design decisions these numbers fed, including a substantial revision of the reference format and the first-cut scope, live in the companion scope doc (7.15-2). One scope note: the probe grew beyond its original spec mid-session, because the reference-format question was reopened while it was being built. It measures several candidate reference arrangements, and all of them are reported here neutrally, including ones the scope doc decides against.*

## 1. Background and terms

The goldens system, in brief. A golden test takes a frozen input (a real game, parsed and committed), runs the real obs and targets pipelines over it, and compares the outputs against frozen reference values that were committed earlier. If the outputs no longer match the references, the test fails (a "fire"), which means the pipeline code's behavior changed. The system's job is to make every behavior change loud and deliberate instead of silent.

Terms used throughout:

- **Fixture**: one real replay from the collected corpus, parsed into a committed npz pair (the sim dict and its meta sidecar). The frozen input.
- **The walk**: replaying the obs pipeline over a fixture tick by tick (`init_memory`, then per tick: `compute_visibility`, `step_memory`, `build_obs`, `build_mask`, and the target functions). The obs memory is path-dependent, so no tick can be skipped. One walk happens per (fixture, config, perspective).
- **Perspective**: the player slot the walk sees the game through. The probe's fixtures record all 8 slots as perspectives. Each perspective walks until its elimination tick, and the winner walks the whole game.
- **Config point**: a frozen obs configuration a golden pins. Two are occupied today: the default (110 channels, fp16 output) and the legacy point (96 channels, fp32, player-status channels off).
- **References** and **blessing**: the references are the frozen expected outputs. Writing them is a "bless". Rewriting them after an intentional behavior change is a "re-bless". An obs-surface change re-blesses every obs reference, because the obs code is shared across fixtures.
- **Directory size vs pack growth**: directory size is what a checkout holds right now. Pack growth is what a re-bless adds to git history permanently (the old reference blobs stay in history forever). Directory size is paid once. Pack growth recurs with every re-bless, which is why it gets its own measurement.
- **Frozen frames**: full obs arrays stored for selected ticks. This was the reference form in the 7.10-2 design. The probe used a provisional event-adjacent pick to size it: t=0, capture ticks and their neighbors, death ticks, the first production tick (t=50), and the last walked frame.
- **Stats layer**: per-frame, per-channel summary statistics, stored as a `[T, C, n_stats]` float32 array. Two stat sets were measured: base (nnz, mean, min, max) and ext (base plus p90 and p99). nnz is the count of non-zero cells in the channel's plane.
- **Hash layers**: 8-byte blake2b digests of exact array bytes. Two granularities were measured: per-frame (the whole `[110, 32, 32]` frame hashed to one value, giving a `[T]` array) and per-frame-per-channel (each channel plane hashed separately, giving `[T, C]`).

What was open before the probe, and why these measurements were worth a build step:

- **Compression** was the design's open variable. The planning envelope was 3:1 to 30:1, extrapolated from parser arrays rather than measured on obs tensors.
- **Runtime** was a flagged risk: "plausibly minutes of pytest" at the planned fixture count, with three mitigation knobs pre-designed (perspective caps, fixture truncation, a fast/full split).
- The planning basis from 7.10-2: roughly 30 fixtures over the project's life, the legacy config on about 3 of them, and an acceptance target of 1 to 2 MiB compressed per fixture.

## 2. Probe setup

The code lives in `packages/training/scripts/goldens_probe/`, in four modules:

- `fixtures.py`: pulls one replay from the collector DB and writes the sim/meta npz pair, mirroring the corpus driver's per-game flow.
- `walk.py`: the numpy-only walk described above, mirroring `IterableDataset._walk` frame for frame (same ordering, same elimination truncation, no torch).
- `layers.py`: builders for the candidate reference layers (stats sets, hash layers, the provisional frozen-frame pick).
- `run_probe.py`: fixture selection, timing, sizing, and the pack-growth simulation.

The first three modules are deliberately written as promotion candidates: if the goldens build wants them, they lift into production code roughly as-is. `run_probe.py` is probe-only. Invocation, from the repo root:

```
uv run python packages/training/scripts/goldens_probe/run_probe.py
```

Options: `--fixtures N`, `--out DIR`, `--skip-pack-sim`. Outputs land in `tmp/goldens-probe/` (gitignored).

Environment: M1 Max (arm64), the uv.lock-pinned venv, numpy 2.4.4, Python 3.14.4.

**Fixture selection.** From the 160,815 candidates in the collector DB that pass the SQL-level corpus filter and have 8 players, the probe picks games at turn-count quantiles (0.2, 0.45, 0.6, 0.85, 0.5) for a spread of lengths. Each pick is verified against the training eligibility filter and the vanilla-FFA wire filter (see §8 for why eligibility matters). The five picks:

| replay | T | board | captures | deaths | neutralizes | sim npz | parse |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LUjb37EhS | 406 | 25×27 | 6 | 7 | 0 | 33.6 KiB | 0.01s |
| icgI8gOFL | 537 | 26×31 | 6 | 7 | 0 | 46.4 KiB | 0.02s |
| GMDaAQ6tU | 625 | 28×31 | 7 | 7 | 0 | 67.2 KiB | 0.02s |

All five have K=8 recorded perspectives, and the meta npz is about 2.3 KiB each.

**Measurement bases.** Timing covers all 8 perspectives of every fixture, and per-frame cost is total seconds over total frames. Sizing uses the longest-lived perspective (typically the winner's full-game walk). The basis matters: an early trial sized artifacts on perspective 0, which happened to die at tick 73 of a 406-tick game, and every size came out roughly 5× too small.

## 3. Runtime

The question: does the full golden suite fit in the fast test tier without any curation?

The walk runs at 2,400 to 3,100 frames per second (both configs, with fp32 legacy slightly faster per frame since it builds fewer channels). A full-length perspective costs 0.13 to 0.32 seconds at the measured game lengths (T=405 to 869). Extrapolated to the planning basis (30 fixtures on the default config plus 3 on legacy):

- about **8 seconds** total at 1 registered perspective per fixture
- about **15 seconds** at 2 perspectives per fixture

Overheads on top of the walk:

- Per-frame hashing adds 54 to 62% of the walk's own time on fp16 stacks, and 105 to 125% on the fp32 legacy stacks (double the bytes to hash). In absolute terms that is 0.1 to 0.3 seconds per walk.
- Per-frame-per-channel hashing costs about 25 to 30% more than per-frame.
- Building the stats layers costs 0.04 to 0.10s (base) or 0.27 to 0.64s (ext, where the percentiles dominate) per fixture. Stats are computed at bless time only, so this never lands in the test path.

Conclusion the numbers support: suite runtime is a non-issue. The three pre-designed mitigation knobs (perspective caps, fixture truncation, fast/full split) are unnecessary at any plausible scale of this system. Caveat: these figures are the walk alone, excluding pytest collection and npz load/decompress, both of which are small but were not measured.

## 4. Storage

The question: what does each candidate reference arrangement cost on disk?

Per fixture, default config (fp16, 110 channels), sized on the winner's walk:

| arrangement | shape | size on disk |
|---|---|---|
| frozen frames, provisional event-adjacent pick | 18–22 frames, full arrays | 40–60 KiB (npz) |
| frozen frames, 2-frame variant (t=0 + last) | 2 frames | 5–6 KiB (npz) |
| full all-frames stack | `[T, 110, 32, 32]` | 0.86–2.83 MiB (npz) |
| stats base | `[T, 110, 4]` f32 | 0.68–1.46 MiB raw / 37–76 KiB npz |
| stats ext | `[T, 110, 6]` f32 | 1.02–2.19 MiB raw / 46–101 KiB npz |
| per-frame hashes | `[T]` u64 | 3.3–6.9 KiB raw |
| per-frame-per-channel hashes | `[T, 110]` u64 | 348–747 KiB raw / 41–94 KiB npz |
| targets, all frames | per-frame scalars + [8]-arrays | 8.6–16.5 KiB (npz) |
| legality-mask stack | `[T, 32, 32, 8]` bool | 20–190 KiB (npz) |
| the fixture npz pair itself | sim + meta | 36–81 KiB |

(The 2-frame variant was an ad-hoc idea added to the probe during the session, not something from the prior design. 7.15-2 discusses it and a stronger successor.)

Extrapolated to the planning basis (default config on 30 fixtures, legacy on 3):

| arrangement | total committed |
|---|---|
| frozen frames, event-adjacent pick | 1.67 MiB |
| full all-frames stacks | 53.87 MiB |
| stats base | 32.82 MiB raw / 1.73 MiB npz |
| stats ext | 49.23 MiB raw / 2.22 MiB npz |
| per-frame-per-channel hashes | 16.41 MiB raw / 2.04 MiB npz |
| per-frame hashes | 159 KiB |
| targets ×30 | 360 KiB |
| legality-mask stacks ×30 | 2.29 MiB |
| fixture npz pairs ×30 | 1.65 MiB |

Observations:

- **Compression far exceeds the planning envelope.** The envelope was 3:1 to 30:1. Measured: 66:1 to 101:1 on fp16 stacks, and 87:1 to 136:1 on fp32 legacy stacks. The cause is the tensor's content: most of the 110 channels are indicators or near-constant planes, and boards are padded to 32×32 with zeros, so DEFLATE has long runs to work with.
- The stats layers compress about 20:1, for the same underlying reason: an indicator channel's stats barely change from frame to frame.
- The per-frame-per-channel hash layer compresses about 8:1 in npz, which looks odd for hash values (they are random-looking bytes). The explanation: static planes such as mountains produce the identical hash value frame after frame, and the compressor picks up the repetition. This redundancy disappears when a re-bless replaces the values with fresh ones (§5).
- The legality-mask stack is the largest targets artifact and the only one that grows with T times board area rather than T times 8.

## 5. Pack growth (the recurring cost)

The question: what does each re-bless add to git history, permanently?

This is a different quantity from §4's directory sizes. A checkout holds one copy of the references. Git history holds every copy ever blessed, and an obs-surface change re-blesses all obs references at once. So the recurring cost of the system is (pack growth per re-bless) × (re-blesses over the project's life), and 7.10-2 §4 flagged it as the budget's real concern.

Method: for each storage format (raw `.npy` vs compressed `.npz`), commit the five fixtures' stats-ext and per-channel-hash layers to a throwaway git repo, then simulate two re-bless scenarios and measure the pack delta after `git gc`. The "targeted" scenario perturbs 5% of frames (stats nudged, those frames' hashes replaced with fresh random values). The "full churn" scenario does the same to 100% of frames.

| | raw `.npy` | compressed `.npz` |
|---|---|---|
| initial pack, 5 fixtures | 624 KiB | 614 KiB |
| targeted re-bless (5% of frames) | +388 KiB | +785 KiB |
| full-churn re-bless | +2.83 MiB | +2.83 MiB |

Scaled to 30 fixtures: a targeted re-bless adds roughly 2.3 MiB (raw) or 4.6 MiB (npz), and a full-churn re-bless adds roughly **17 MiB** in either format.

How to read these numbers:

- **Git compresses stored objects itself**, which is why the raw files (about 10 MiB on disk) and the npz files pack to the same ~0.6 MiB of history. Storing raw costs nothing extra in history. It does cost checkout size: about 60 MiB of working tree at 30 fixtures, vs about 4 MiB for npz.
- **Raw delta-compresses better under small changes.** When 95% of a file's bytes are unchanged, git stores the new raw version as a small delta against the old blob. Compressing first scrambles the byte alignment (one changed value perturbs the whole DEFLATE stream), so npz pays about 2× on targeted changes.
- **The full-churn number is almost entirely the per-channel hash layer.** About 2.5 of the 2.83 MiB is the five fixtures' worth of fresh hash values (roughly 500 KiB each), which are incompressible random bytes. Format cannot help, and the §4 repetition trick is gone because the values are all new. Scaled up, the per-channel hash layer alone accounts for ~15 MiB of the ~17 MiB per full re-bless.
- For comparison, the same arithmetic on other arrangements: a full re-bless of per-frame hashes alone is about 160 KiB, of the stats-ext npz layer about 2.2 MiB, and of full all-frames stacks about 50 MiB.

Caveat: replacing hashes with random values models the worst case. A real change produces new hash values only for frames whose bytes actually changed, so a real re-bless lands between the targeted and full-churn figures, depending on how widely the change reaches.

## 6. Determinism evidence

The question: is the walk bit-reproducible? Exact-equality references (and hashes most of all) assume that the same fixture and the same code produce the same bytes, every run.

The strongest evidence arrived by accident. The pack simulation's second run mistakenly reused the first run's git repos, and every commit measured +0 KiB of growth: git's content addressing found every blob byte-identical to the first run's. Those blobs were the stats arrays (fp32 means and percentiles) and hash layers computed over full obs stacks. In other words, two independent process invocations, hours apart, produced bit-identical results through the entire chain: DB wire blob → parse → sim → walk → fp16 obs → stats → hashes. The simulation was then fixed to use fresh repos per run (deterministic content plus a reused repo yields false zeros), and the third run reproduced the first run's numbers exactly.

Two pre-existing pieces of evidence point the same way:

- The replay-parser fixture tests byte-compare committed npz against freshly regenerated output, and have stayed green across months of runs.
- The obs-path parity test asserts exact array equality between two independently implemented obs paths, at every tick and every perspective.

## 7. The np.log cross-platform experiment

The question: the obs pipeline's integer and boolean operations, and its basic float operations (add, multiply, divide, casts: all correctly rounded by IEEE-754 specification), reproduce bit-identically across platforms. That leaves the 16 log-scaled channels, fed by the pipeline's one transcendental (`np.log`), as the only cross-platform-fragile part. How big are real `np.log` variations in practice?

Method, runnable as-is:

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.arange(1, 20001)                       # realistic army values
x = (1.0 + v).astype(np.float32)
y = np.log(x)
y_ref = np.log(x.astype(np.float64)).astype(np.float32)   # correctly-rounded reference
ulp_err = np.abs(y.view(np.int32) - y_ref.view(np.int32))
print((ulp_err == 0).mean())                  # 1.0: correctly rounded on all inputs

h  = y.astype(np.float16)
up = np.nextafter(y, np.float32(np.inf)).astype(np.float16)
dn = np.nextafter(y, np.float32(-np.inf)).astype(np.float16)
print(((h != up) | (h != dn)).mean())         # 0.00035: fp16 flip rate under ±1 ulp jitter
```

Results on this platform (M1, numpy 2.4.4):

- numpy's fp32 log is **correctly rounded on all 20,000 inputs** (0 ulp error against the fp64 reference).
- If another platform differed by ±1 fp32 ulp, the fp16 downcast would flip the stored value for only **1 in ~2,857** inputs (0.035%). The rest round to the same fp16 value.
- Scale context: 1 fp32 ulp at log-values 2 / 5 / 9 is 2.4e-7 / 4.8e-7 / 9.5e-7 in absolute terms. One fp16 grid step at the same values is 2.0e-3 / 3.9e-3 / 7.8e-3, roughly 4,000× coarser.

Interpretation. A cross-platform difference requires the other platform's log to miss correct rounding, since this one doesn't. The worst realistic case is a ±1 ulp disagreement, which is around 1e-7 in relative terms (far below anything training or eval can see), and the fp16 references absorb all but ~0.035% of even those. The expected cross-platform signature is therefore zero to single-digit flipped fp16 values per fixture, confined to log-scaled channels. The fp32 legacy references have no downcast to absorb anything, so they would show every misrounded value densely (the canary behavior 7.10-2 §4 described).

Caveats: this measures one side of a two-platform comparison (the other side needs a second machine, and the tiny hash references make that a cheap future experiment). It covers one transform shape over integer army values. And numpy falls back to the scalar system libm on non-contiguous arrays, which would reintroduce platform variation the SIMD path avoids.

## 8. Incidental findings

**Goldens fixtures must come from the training-eligible population.** Training only consumes games that pass `filters.is_eligible`: player count exactly 8 (`ELIGIBLE_PLAYER_COUNT`), board side at most 32 (`MAX_BOARD_SIDE`), and T at most 2000 (`MAX_GAME_LENGTH`). The probe found this the hard way: its first trial picked a 4-player game and `step_memory` crashed indexing `sim["initial_generals"][p]` with `p` ranging over 8 slots. The dataset walk never pads the generals array, because the corpus filter guarantees it never sees a game with fewer than 8 players (padding exists only on the live-inference path, via `pad_initial_generals`). Consequence: the goldens' fixture corpus query needs the same eligibility gate, and the probe now applies it during selection.

**Neutralize events are scarce.** One of the five sampled games contained neutralize events (two of them), while every game had 5 to 7 captures and 7 deaths. Small sample, but the direction is clear: capture and death coverage comes free with any full game, and the fixture corpus query must target neutralizes explicitly.

## 9. Caveats and validity scope

- All numbers are from one machine (M1 Max) on the pinned environment. The determinism evidence (§6) is same-platform evidence only.
- Five fixtures is a small sample of the corpus. The per-fixture ranges above reflect these five games, not the corpus distribution's tails. Games near the T=2000 eligibility ceiling would scale the T-proportional artifacts (stats, hashes, mask stacks, walk time) by roughly 2 to 3× over the longest game measured.
- Walk timings exclude pytest collection and npz loading. Hash and stats timings were measured on assembled stacks after the walk, not interleaved with it.
- The frozen-frame pick rule used for sizing is probe-grade (a plausible approximation of the designed rule, never reviewed).
- The pack simulation's perturbation model brackets rather than predicts: random hash replacement is the worst case for the changed fraction.
- A numpy upgrade or platform change invalidates §6 and §7's platform-specific claims. Both are named events in the goldens design, with their own procedures.
