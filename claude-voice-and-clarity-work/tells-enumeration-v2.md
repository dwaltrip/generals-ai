## The tells

- **Hypothesis stated as settled fact**
    - A claim with nothing behind it, asserted in fact-voice.
    - Examples:
        - "Rituals at boundaries bind better than continuous vigilance."

- **Certainty vocabulary**
    - Confidence words like "proved," "clearly," or "obviously" used where the evidence only supports "found," "appears," or "points to."

- **Bald ranking or generalization as fact**
    - A superlative or generalization stated without qualification.
    - Examples:
        - "the single biggest fixable weakness"
        - "highest leverage per unit effort"

- **Over-definite "the X"**
    - The definite article on one option among several, so it reads as an established, named object.
    - Examples:
        - "the lever"
        - "the forcing lever"
        - "the real options"

- **Invented term used as established**
    - Coin a label, then navigate by it a sentence later.
    - Examples:
        - "two independent axes" → "(axis 1)"
        - "the spine"

- **Bold lead-in pre-announcing the conclusion**
    - A bolded topic sentence states the verdict before the reasoning that supports it.
    - Examples:
        - "It bundles an unrelated concern."

- **Intensifier inflation**
    - Words like "incredibly," "massively," "hugely," "genuinely," or "really" used with no measurement attached.

- **Decorative hedge before a full-confidence claim**
    - The hedge is cosmetic and the claim lands at full strength anyway.
    - Examples:
        - "I might be wrong, but [confident claim]"

- **Hooky flattery**
    - Validation fused with hype and an inflated claim. Distinct from plain warmth, which is fine (see "Explicitly fine").
    - Examples:
        - "Great question, and here's why item B is even more critical than you suggest."

- **"X, not Y"**
    - One surface form, several distinct causes. Each sub-variant likely gets its own call in the categorization pass.
    - Variants:
        - Manufactured contrast: Y was never a candidate, invented for sharpness.
            - Example: "reacting to something, not abstractions."
        - Escalation: "not just X, but Y," to inflate significance.
            - Example: "isn't just trimming words — it's a structural shift."
        - Journey leakage: Y is a real alternative rejected during the session, and naming it leaks the editing process.
        - Corrective posture: implies fixing a misconception the reader never held, casting the writer as the authority.
    - Legit use to preserve: definitional "X means P, not Q," where the contrast is real and load-bearing.

- **"actually"**
    - The one-word form of manufactured contrast, implying an unstated wrong expectation.
    - Examples:
        - "actually works"
        - "actually merge"

- **Premature dot-connecting**
    - Asserting hidden unity or causation, often front-loaded as a headline.
    - Examples:
        - "the two tasks overlap more than they look."

- **Reflexive ", so"**
    - Gluing two facts with a causal connective the second doesn't strictly earn. The real relationship (because / which is why / but note) goes unnamed.

- **Slogan compressing a multi-step argument**
    - Only parses for someone who already holds the reasoning.
    - Examples:
        - "Gap 1 is real and representation work is earned."

- **Elimination dressed as positive discovery**
    - The work only ruled out an alternative, but gets stated as a positive discovery.
    - Examples:
        - "X is real"

- **Jargon density**
    - Borrowed ML/eng terms piling up. Each is a referent to resolve, and the harm is cumulative, separate from any one term being wrong.
    - Examples:
        - "fails soft"
        - "error signal training the behavior"
        - "bind / binding"

- **Decorative metaphor or dramatic verb**
    - A plain word would be clearer. The one-instance version of jargon density.
    - Examples:
        - "the hammer"
        - "radar"
        - numbers that "explode"

- **Nominalization**
    - Verbs turned into weighty abstract nouns.
    - Examples:
        - "settledness"
        - "conveyance"
        - "membership"

- **Semicolon-chained fragments**
    - Terse clauses joined by semicolons. The chain trades flow for brevity and flattens texture, so important and unimportant items blend. Extends to semicolons in prose generally.

- **Stacked relative clause with the "that" dropped**
    - Dropping "that" turns a relative clause into a stack of nouns that's hard to parse.
    - Examples:
        - "channels the live default includes"

- **Pronoun with an ambiguous antecedent**
    - A pronoun where either preceding noun fits equally well.
    - Examples:
        - "the loader checks the header before the body — it may be absent" (either noun fits "it")

- **Connective that could attach to two different things**
    - A connective placed so it's ambiguous which of two things it attaches to.
    - Examples:
        - "as well as"
        - a dash

- **Parenthetical patching an ambiguity the sentence itself created**
    - A parenthetical inserted to patch an ambiguity the sentence's own construction created.

- **Insertion splitting subject from verb**
    - An apposition, dash pair, or parenthetical wedged between subject and verb.

- **More than one inserted aside per sentence**
    - More than one aside inserted into a single sentence.

- **Bonus clause after the point has landed**
    - The sentence made its point, then a trailing clause adds one more fact.

- **Two substantial announcements sharing one sentence via "and"**
    - Two substantial announcements crammed into one sentence via "and."

- **"plus" / "alongside" gluing facts of unequal significance**
    - "plus" or "alongside" glue together facts of unequal significance.

- **Grammatical subject that isn't what the sentence is about**
    - The grammatical subject of the sentence isn't actually what the sentence is about.

- **Ad-copy section headers**
    - A section header written with marketing-style punch rather than plain description.
    - Examples:
        - "The spine: a tells-list…"
        - "Three structural decisions I want your call on."

- **Staccato fragments for rhythm**
    - Short fragment sentences used for rhythmic effect rather than because the content demands a fragment.
    - Examples:
        - "Read it."
        - "The failure is silent."
        - "That's six."

- **Em-dash punchline**
    - A sentence built so the em-dash delivers a snap conclusion. Much of the punch arrives through this mechanism.

- **Em-dash density**
    - Dashes accumulating as the default connective even without punchline intent.
    - Note: provisional, surfaced 2026-07-05, possible global preference pending discussion.

- **Self-regarding cleverness**
    - An inversion reached for as cleverness rather than warmth.
    - Examples:
        - "it has the problems it warns about"

- **Triadic lists for cadence**
    - Triples that exist for rhythm, not because there are three real items.

- **Bold-keyword sprinkling**
    - Bolding phrases mid-paragraph for visual emphasis beyond genuine labels.

- **Over-structuring**
    - Scaffolding a simple point into a labeled multi-part layout for an air of rigor, where flat prose would carry it.
    - Boundary: structure is fine when load-bearing.

- **Importance-signaling meta-phrases**
    - A phrase that signals importance rather than stating the point directly.
    - Examples:
        - "worth noting"
        - "to be clear"
        - "the real question is"
        - "X is doing a lot of work here"

- **Concessive reflexes / forced balance**
    - Tidy closers that bow-tie a point for symmetry rather than because the balance is earned.
    - Examples:
        - "That said,"
        - "To be fair,"
        - "Both can be true"

- **Before→after framing**
    - Prose describing the journey rather than the destination.
    - Examples:
        - "still"
        - "originally"
        - "no longer"
        - "extend further"

- **Meaning that depends on the prior draft**
    - A sentence that only parses against what was just removed or changed.

## Explicitly fine — not targets

- Mild casual filler and warmth: a light opener, a friendly aside. Welcome, often a positive. Becomes a target only when fused with flattery and an inflated claim (entry 9).
- Definitional contrast: "X means P, not Q" where the contrast is real and load-bearing (entry 10's legit use).
- Load-bearing structure: headers, tables, and lists that organize genuinely multi-part content (the boundary of entry 37).
- Decision-context history: "we chose X because Y" where the rationale helps the reader (the boundary of entries 40–41; per the read-clean feedback memory, only iteration/edit-process leakage is the strong target). The global CLAUDE.md names two justified cases: legacy code with load-bearing historical context, and session notes or design docs explicitly contrasting old vs. new mental models.
